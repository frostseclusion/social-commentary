We have grown so used to the sheer wretchedness of the Chinese pop music scene that it no longer surprises us. Calling out that wretchedness has, by now, become its own orthodoxy — the safe, correct position everyone is expected to hold. We stand thigh-deep in the muck of Mandopop, trading spit and curses by day, and by night grieving the endlessly deferred revival of Chinese-language pop. In this endless war, people who know full well it’s futile keep throwing themselves in, while somehow the audience has been elevated above judgment. They convict everyone else and leave themselves off the charge sheet. The moment anyone dares point this out, the response is swift and merciless: *The audience has done nothing wrong; it’s the entire industry that’s to blame!* Even the bravest would think twice before walking into that barrage. And yet, for all the absolution granted to our dear listeners — who, day after day, rain blows on everyone in sight — the industry has not improved one bit. When things have come to such a pass, how can we not question the supposedly self-evident axiom: “the audience is innocent”?

I don’t intend to spend a whole essay proving *why* the audience is not innocent. Many have tried before, and most have come to little good. The standard arguments on my side have grown so familiar to the other camp that they’ve lost most of their force. We may have to admit that the divide between those who believe the audience innocent and those who don’t is simply too wide to be bridged by yet another lengthy debate. That said, there is one thing I can still do: as someone firmly convinced that the audience is not blameless, I can point to which groups of listeners strike me as least innocent of all. In other words, I’m not going to force readers to share my conclusion — I’m just going to present some examples. Whether these examples are guilty, and whether their guilt is enough to show — at least in part — that criticizing the audience is necessary, I leave to you.

## I. Worship the Old, Scorn the New — At Any Age

First in line are those who worship the past and disparage the present. I say “at any age” because this tendency is by no means limited to older listeners; if anything, the peculiar ecosystem of Chinese pop has made it even more pronounced among young people, outstripping their elders. To be fair, the reason listeners of all generations so uniformly believe that “older Chinese music was simply better” ultimately stems from the failure of the new generation to deliver. Among the newer acts who have managed to go mainstream, none can hold a candle to the kings and queens of yesteryear. Meanwhile, those with talent and originality struggle to gain commercial recognition. But none of that justifies the sheer shamelessness with which so many listeners indulge in their nostalgia.

How far do these past-worshippers go? Their breadth of listening is likely the thinnest of any group — yet they are among the most eager to pontificate and play the critic. I have a perfect example. The Bilibili creator @HOPICO runs one of the best music media channels on the Chinese internet, especially since launching the HMA (HOPICO Music Awards), which honors the best Chinese-language albums each year. Just as viewers were delighting in discovering hidden gems, in shuffled the nostalgia brigade — young, middle-aged, old alike — trailing the rotten stench of the grave, green fluid seeping from the corners of their mouths as they struggled to raise their crumbling skeletal arms and declare:

> “You can barely scrape together ten good Chinese songs, so picking forty good albums is honestly impressive.”
>
> “Feels completely disconnected from pop music. I’ve never heard of a single one of these albums or artists.”
>
> “If it’s pop music, why isn’t it popular?!”
>
> “Honestly I’m only here for HOPICO’s video editing and Zhou Yang (author’s note: HOPICO’s host). I don’t know any of these nominees or winners, and I’m not going to listen to them either. I’m past the age of listening to ‘new songs’ and don’t really care to learn.”
>
> “What am I supposed to do with this? It all feels way too niche. Doesn’t even qualify as pop.”
>
> “What’s the point if they’re all niche albums?”
>
> “This is basically just a niche-music gala, an alternative-pop gathering.”

(All of the above are quoted verbatim, except for the author’s note identifying Zhou Yang, from the comment sections of HOPICO’s two videos on the 2025 HMA.)

Some go even further, openly sneering: “Honestly, not as good as…” followed by the name of a Taiwanese pop star who blew up in the 2000s.

Can listeners like these help Chinese music move forward? Shouldn’t people who write such comments be told, in no uncertain terms, to get lost?

The eras these worshippers idolize are mostly the Cantopop and Mandopop waves of the 1970s and 1980s, or the millennial R&B boom of the 1990s and 2000s. But whichever era they revere, they all seem oblivious to the fact that this is merely their personal taste — taste that cannot and should not be used to dictate anything to anyone. The HMA spans pop, alternative, R&B, hip-hop, rock, jazz, electronic, and more. How did a bunch of old pop stars who made some decent records decades ago endow these listeners with the confidence to look down on — and lecture, with the sour air of a pedantic scholar — newcomers whose albums they haven’t even bothered to hear? The genres don’t even line up. Were those old kings and queens musical polymaths?

Of course, a crueler truth: these past-worshippers probably don’t even know what “genre” means.

Older Chinese pop is genuinely wonderful; I love it too. But humanity must eventually face forward. The very people who love to drone on about how “the Chinese music scene is regressing instead of progressing” are themselves the most stubborn, the most resistant to growth, the most thoroughly ossified of all.

I have nothing more to say.

## II. Lyrics Above All, Musicianship Be Damned

Second are those who listen only to the lyrics and couldn’t care less about the music itself, no matter how shoddily it’s put together.

Even I hesitate to be too harsh here, because this tendency is rooted deep in Chinese culture.

The Chinese have long excelled at creating and savoring the beauty of poetry and verse. Even in the case of Song *ci* and Yuan *qu*, what survives today is the text, not the musical scores that once accompanied them (I’m not saying this cultural trait *caused* the scores to be lost, of course). Perhaps driven by this historical inertia, Chinese audiences have become especially prone to treating music as a form of literature, exalting works that expend great effort on the words rather than on musicality.

What has this led to? China was already a latecomer to modern popular music, and this fixation on lyrical aesthetics has effectively held its development hostage. I’m not saying that songs with beautiful lyrics have no value. I’m saying that for a very long time, we haven’t truly prioritized the development of music *as music*: the development of composition, arrangement, production, theory, and so on. So when Western pop entered the Chinese market and began its conquest, Chinese pop was naturally helpless to resist. You may love the beauty of words, but the ear is not an organ for reading. If people are so passionate about crafting tear-jerking lines, I suggest they go write modern poetry. Why bother with music at all?

Let me be clear: I’m not a Western-centrist. The unfortunate reality is that the global pop ecosystem is built on a foundation of Western music theory. I have no doubt that, at some ripe moment in the future, China will develop its own distinctive pop tradition — one that holds lyrical beauty as its highest ideal. But that isn’t what listeners currently expect from Mandopop. What most people want is for Chinese pop to go toe-to-toe with Western pop, so my vision isn’t realistic yet. As long as the goal is still to do what Japan once did — catch up and then surpass — we have no choice but to take the music itself seriously, instead of staring at the lyrics all day.

The exaltation of lyrics has also spawned a wave of counterfeits. Far too much pop music is garbage from compositional logic to production quality, yet it gets crowned a masterpiece simply because the lyrics are written in classical Chinese, or semi-classical Chinese, or because a traditional Chinese instrument shows up for a few bars, or because a pentatonic phrase appears now and then. The same illness afflicts hip-hop: let a rapper say something about society and politics, and he is instantly elevated to the status of Lu Xun, regardless of how weak his flow and beat actually are by the standards of the genre. It is precisely this indifference to the music itself, this absence of genuine musical judgment, that allows mediocrity to pass for substance.

## III. The Persecuted “I’m Just a Commoner”

The third group I want to call out overlaps considerably with the first.

These people suffer from a widespread persecution complex. The symptoms: you’re having a perfectly normal discussion about music; you mention a concept they don’t understand, or simply name an album they’ve never heard; and they instantly trigger their defense mechanisms:

> “Oh, here comes Mr. Niche.”
>
> “Look at that superiority complex.”
>
> “If ordinary people like it, who the hell do you think you are?”

Then they turn around and accuse you of aesthetic bullying.

If the past-worshippers never look down, these people never look up. A perfectly ordinary exchange about music, and they feel personally attacked. When in doubt, they play the humble card — *I’m just an ordinary person who doesn’t know anything* — and throw themselves to the ground, covering their ears. It’s a pathetic, spineless version of “I was but a commoner from Huaiyou.”

But who is persecuting them, really? It all stems from a deep-seated insecurity. They are indeed lacking in knowledge and narrow in perspective; they can’t follow the references other people make. But unwilling to concede any ground, they dismiss everything the other person says — including things that are common knowledge or must-listens within music circles — as mere posturing, mere attempts at superiority.

These people aren’t here to talk about music. They’re here to pick a fight. I don’t understand why someone bringing up something you don’t know counts as “bullying.” By that logic, a primary school teacher teaching multiplication is guilty of “knowledge bullying,” and parents teaching life lessons are guilty of “experience bullying.”

The point is simple: no one can stop you from growing except yourself. Why not treat these moments as opportunities — to encounter something new, to learn a concept you didn’t know, to discover an album you might have missed? No one is born knowing everything, but there will always be someone who knows more than you. So why not try to learn a little more, even if only to have more to talk about over tea? Why be so thin-skinned and quick to take offense?

Dear readers, ask yourselves again: are listeners like these truly innocent?

## IV. Perhaps I’m Just Fretting in Vain

After all that, I can’t rule out one possibility: every criticism and concern I’ve raised is meaningless, and I’m simply fretting in vain.

Maybe not as many people actually care about the direction of Mandopop as we think — they just join in because everyone else is piling on. Maybe not that many people want to encounter new things or become better listeners; to them, music is just a light pastime, one among many. The Chinese music scene is far from thriving, but it hasn’t been declared dead either, and the talented musicians I know can still put food on the table.

But I believe that we all absorb an endless stream of input every day, and eventually something has to come back out. I have spent eight years with music, and there are things I simply am not willing to let slide. If I too keep silent, then I too must bear the responsibility and the cost of that silence. In this world, there is no such thing as a truly disinterested spectator.

Thank you for reading this far.
